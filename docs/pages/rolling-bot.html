Rolling clock bot

A little robot that rolls on the whiteboard itself, drawing as it goes. A spherical wheel holds it at a constant height (so no lift motor), and the marker tilts one way to draw, the other way to erase — the way a hand holds a pen. This page shows it from the top (rolling and drawing a clock) and the side (the tilt and the ball), and works out which drive is most effective.

Which drive is most effective?

"Effective" here means five things, weighted: fidelity (can it draw any direction and tight curves — 35%), accuracy (how far it drifts over a clock — 30%), simplicity (motor count and build — 20%), pen-height hold (constant contact — 15%), and draw time. Simulated by planning the clock path (ink 1341 mm, pen-up moves 1884 mm, total travel 3226 mm) and modelling each drive's slip and holonomy.

Drift is the decider, and it's brutal for a clock that redraws for hours.

With no position reference, odometry error grows with distance travelled — 32–97 mm over a single clock, so it walks off the board. The fix is to re-home against the board edges every redraw (drive into two perpendicular edges to zero position and heading), which bounds drift to one stroke's worth (~9–24 mm). A camera watching the bot closes the loop properly. The fixed arm has none of this — its angles are absolute, so it never drifts, which is why it tops the table. Among rolling drives, a holonomic one (3-omni, or a driven ball) wins on drawing quality; pure differential is simplest but can't do the curves and ticks cleanly.

What the simulation measured

DRIFT
slip × travel. The clock is ~3.2 m of rolling, so even 1% slip = 32 mm wander. The killer metric.
HOLONOMY
Can it move any direction without turning? Holonomic drives draw smooth curves; differential must arc or stop-turn.
PATH OVERHEAD
Pen-up moves (1884 mm) exceed ink (1341 mm), so stroke ordering matters — start from one side and sweep across.
HEIGHT HOLD
The ball keeps the chassis a fixed height off the board, so pressure is set by tilt alone — the lift motor disappears.
MOTORS
Fewer is simpler and lighter (less to drift). Differential 2, omni/ball 3, mecanum 4 — plus one tilt servo each.
TIME
~20–36 s per clock. All are well within a minute, so speed isn't the constraint — accuracy is.

The tilt, the ball, and no lift motor

Holding the marker a few degrees off vertical (a trailing angle, like handwriting) draws a cleaner line than straight-down — it won't dig in or skip, and it tolerates small height changes. Tilting the other way brings the eraser end of the marker down. Because the spherical wheel fixes the ride height, the single tilt servo does both jobs — draw, erase, and lift — so you drop the separate Z motor your earlier designs needed. The side view shows it: watch the contact point and the tilt flip when you switch modes.

This already exists — borrow from it

The closest prior art is iRobot Root (Harvard Wyss, patent US11529740B2) — a bot that rolls on a vertical magnetic whiteboard and does exactly your tilt-to-draw, tilt-to-erase with one motor turning a double-ended arm through three states (pen down, neutral, eraser down) with an angle sensor. Credit it in the pitch, and copy two tricks: put the pen at the robot's centre of rotation (so turning on the spot leaves a dot, not a smear) and offset the eraser to the rear (so turning while erasing doesn't wipe ahead of where it's going).

Rolling efficiently: start from one side

Every millimetre the bot rolls costs time and drift, and the pen-up moves between strokes are more than half the travel. So order the strokes as a sweep from one edge across to the other (and draw the ring in one continuous loop), instead of hopping around. For the clock that cuts backtracking and keeps the wheel rolling one way — which also means less slip reversal, so less drift. The top view draws in that swept order.

Recommendation

If the clock must redraw reliably all evening, the fixed arm wins on paper (zero drift) — but if you want the rolling bot for its whole-board reach and the elegant ball-plus-tilt, build a holonomic drive (3-omni, or two drive wheels plus a ball caster) with Root-style edge re-homing every cycle, and if you can, an overhead camera with AprilTags on the bot and the four corners (as CMU did) for a true closed loop. Keep the marker on one tilt servo over the ball, pen at the centre of rotation, eraser offset to the rear. And if the demo allows, run the board flat on a table — the patent's biggest drift term is gravity on a vertical board, and flat removes it. Differential is quickest to build and fine for a loose one-off drawing, but it fights you on curves and can't hold a clock in place without help.
