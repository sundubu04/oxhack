Clock arm simulator

A five-bar drawing arm — two motors at the base, a light linkage reaching to the pen — drawing on your 35 × 35 cm whiteboard. The pen tip rides on a rolling ball that keeps it at a constant height as the arm sweeps, and the marker tilts to lift, draw and erase — so there's no lift motor, and no fragile elbow motor. Dexterity is the transmission angle between the two linkage arms at the pen (1 = best, 0 = the five-bar's singularity where they line up). Keep the drawing in the green zone. Show tip-error wobble jitters the drawn line by the actuator's error so you can see what that size board really looks like.

Is it feasible on a 35 cm board?

Short answer: yes for a loose, hand-drawn look; borderline for a crisp clock with bare hobby servos.

A five-bar needs ~24 cm links to cover a 35 cm board, so the pen sits up to ~30 cm from a motor. Tip error is lever × servo step, so a small angle error becomes millimetres at the pen:

ActuatorAngle stepTip error @ 300 mm leverGood for
Hobby servo (SG90)~1°~5.2 mmloose line art only
Digital metal-gear (MG996R)~0.3°~1.6 mmchunky clock, wobbly detail
Servo + 3:1 belt/gear reduction~0.1°~0.5 mma clean, readable clock
Stepper + microstep + belt~0.05°~0.2 mmcrisp, best repeatability

So the board size is fine — the limit is the actuator, not the reach. For a Maarten-Baas clock, use belt/gear reduction or steppers. Shrinking the links helps too: optimise for the clock disk, not the whole square, so the lever is shorter and the error smaller. Near a singularity (worst dexterity ≈ 0.4) both error and joint speed amplify ~2.5×, so keep the drawing in the dexterous band. Note the rolling ball only fixes the height (Z) — consistent contact and pressure, no skipping — it does not fix the X-Y error above, which comes from the arm's joint angles. Extra mitigations: draw flat on a table (gravity only adds droop, not sag) rather than vertical, and add a camera closed-loop correction if you want crisp lines from cheap servos. Prior art agrees: Plotclock uses tiny 35/55 mm links for exactly this reason, and a Cornell rebuild got unreadable digits from servo error at larger scale.

We optimise manipulability (|sin| of the transmission angle). Optimising isotropy instead (equal precision in all directions) favours slightly unequal links (La/Lb ≈ 1/√2); we keep equal links because it maximises the worst-case transmission and simplifies the build.

Why the five-bar (not two serial arms)

A serial 2-link arm puts a motor at the elbow — heavy, wobbly, and it fights gravity at the end of a long arm. The five-bar keeps both motors bolted to the base and reaches with a light linkage (the Line-us / Plotclock design), so it's far stiffer at this size. At the tip, a rolling ball rides on the board and holds the marker at a constant height as the arm drags it around — so the arm never needs a precise Z, the pen can't dig in or skip, and board bumps are absorbed. The marker sits on one tilt servo: tilt it a little to draw (a trailing angle, like a hand), tilt to centre to lift, tilt the other way to bring the eraser down. That one servo replaces the whole lift axis. The trade: a five-bar has its own singularity (when the two linkage arms line up) and a lens-shaped workspace, both shown live on the left.

What software hands the hardware

Software owns point → the two motor angles; the Pi owns angle → servo. The third output is the marker tilt: a draw angle (pen down), a centre angle (lifted — the ball still carries the weight), and an erase angle (eraser down). Pressure is how firm the draw tilt is. We write our own IK (Plotclock is CC-BY, Makelangelo GPL-2.0, GRBL GPL-3.0 — read, don't copy; check the event's licence rule).

The maths (verified)

// five-bar IK: pen (x,y) -> motor angles a (left), b (right). Each side is a 2R solve.
for each base O in { A=(-d/2,0) sign -1,  B=(+d/2,0) sign +1 }:
  dx=x-O.x; dy=y-O.y; r=hypot(dx,dy);
  if (r > La+Lb || r < |La-Lb|) return OUT_OF_REACH;
  c = (r² + La² - Lb²) / (2·La·r);
  angle = atan2(dy,dx) + sign·acos(c);        // the motor angle
  elbow = O + La·(cos angle, sin angle);
dexterity = |sin(angle between (elbowA-pen) and (elbowB-pen))|;  // transmission

Each elbow sits exactly La from its motor and Lb from the pen by construction; checked over the drawing disk. Tip error from one motor ≈ (La+Lb)·Δθ in the worst case.
