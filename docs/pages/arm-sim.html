Clock arm simulator

A two-link arm — a base joint and an elbow joint, two arms in series — drawing a 20 cm clock centred on your 35 × 35 cm whiteboard (it doesn't fill the board; the margin is deliberate, and keeps the arm small). The pen tip rides on a rolling ball that holds a constant height, and the marker tilts to lift, draw and erase — no lift motor. The arm (160 mm links) stays small, so small motors. Dexterity is |sin θ₂|: 1 (green) where the elbow is near 90° and the arm is most accurate, 0 (red) at the singularities — fully folded (inner edge) or fully stretched (outer edge). Keep the whole board green-ish. Optimise sets equal links to put the board in the sweet band.

Link length for a centred clock (least motor torque)

You don't need to cover the whole board, so the arm only has to reach the clock — but centring the clock pushes it to the middle of the board, so the links are a little longer than for a clock tucked near the base. Base mounted ~30 mm below the board, centred; equal links are optimal:

Recommendation: a 200 mm clock centred → L1 = L2 = 160 mm, base 30 mm below the board. It leaves ~7.5 cm of margin all round, uses only ~40% of the torque of a whole-board arm (small, cheap motors, no sag), and the short lever keeps tip error low. Equal links are optimal (for a fixed L1²+L2² the product L1·L2, which sets dexterity, peaks when they're equal). A bigger centred clock drops the near-edge dexterity (its bottom nears the base), so raise the standoff if you go past 200 mm. The spec sheet has the print dimensions.

The mechanism — two arms, no M

A shoulder joint at the base turns link 1; an elbow joint turns link 2; the pen is at the end — a plain serial two-link arm. The honest trade is the elbow motor: sitting at the end of link 1, its weight makes the arm heavier and wobblier than a base-only design. Two ways to handle it:

Elbow driveHowTrade
Direct servo at the elbowA servo bolted at the elbow turns link 2Simplest; adds tip mass and flex — fine with light links and a slow draw
Parallelogram from the base (anglepoise)A second base motor drives link 2's angle through a parallel bar, so both motors sit at the baseStill looks like two arms, no M — and no heavy elbow motor. A little more linkage

Either way the control is the same 2-link inverse kinematics below (base angle θ₁, elbow angle θ₂). At the tip, a rolling ball rides on the board and holds the marker at a constant height — so the arm never needs a precise Z, the pen can't dig in or skip, and board bumps are absorbed. The marker sits on one tilt servo: tilt a little to draw, tilt to centre to lift, tilt the other way to bring the eraser down (as iRobot's Root does). That one servo replaces the whole lift axis.

What software hands the hardware

Software owns point → base and elbow angle; the Pi owns angle → servo. The third output is the marker tilt: a draw angle (pen down), a centre angle (lifted — the ball still carries the weight), and an erase angle (eraser down). Pressure is how firm the draw tilt is. The maths (verified)

// inverse kinematics: target (x,y) -> base θ₁, elbow θ₂
r2 = x*x + y*y;  r = sqrt(r2);
if (r > L1+L2 || r < |L1-L2|) return OUT_OF_REACH;
c2 = (r2 - L1² - L2²) / (2·L1·L2);
θ₂ = atan2( ±sqrt(1-c2²), c2 );                 // + elbow-up, − elbow-down
θ₁ = atan2(y, x) - atan2(L2·sin θ₂, L1 + L2·cos θ₂);
dexterity = |sin θ₂|;  best at θ₂ = ±90°, zero at 0° and 180°.

Forward∘inverse round-trips to ~1e-13 mm over the whole board; out-of-range returns cleanly. Fourier mode runs a DFT on the path and redraws it from the N largest epicycles.

Is it accurate enough on a 35 cm board?

Yes for a loose, hand-drawn look; borderline for a crisp clock with bare hobby servos.

Tip error is lever × servo step, and a 250 mm arm has a long lever, so a small angle error becomes millimetres at the pen:

ActuatorAngle stepTip error @ 300 mm leverGood for
Hobby servo (SG90)~1°~5.2 mmloose line art only
Digital metal-gear (MG996R)~0.3°~1.6 mmchunky clock, wobbly detail
Servo + 3:1 belt/gear reduction~0.1°~0.5 mma clean, readable clock
Stepper + microstep + belt~0.05°~0.2 mmcrisp, best repeatability

The board size is fine — the limit is the actuator. For a Maarten-Baas clock use belt/gear reduction or steppers. The rolling ball only fixes the height (Z) — consistent contact, pressure and no skipping — it does not fix the X-Y error above, which comes from the joint angles. Other mitigations: draw flat on a table (gravity only adds droop, not sag), keep the drawing in the dexterous band (near the singularity both error and joint speed amplify ~2.5×), and add a camera closed-loop if you want crisp lines from cheap servos.
