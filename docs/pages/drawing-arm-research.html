Drawing arm research

A robot that writes the time on a whiteboard and wipes it is twelve years old, so the clock is our joke, not our claim. What nobody has built is an arm that hears you, decides what to draw, and draws it fast and exactly from one Fourier path. And there is a published clock-drawing test that language models fail and we can try to pass.

Why a live model API can't hold the pen

  • Nothing to wire up. Models that output motor actions (Gemini Robotics 2, its on-device version, the Pi API) are waitlist or tester only; OpenAI has no robotics API. The public Gemini Robotics-ER 2 returns text points on a 0–1000 grid.
  • Imprecise. On Point-Bench, where any point inside the object counts, GPT-4o scored 29.5% and Gemini 2.5 Flash 46.8%. A clock needs a true circle.
  • Not repeatable. One prompt run 1,000 times at temperature 0 gave 80 different answers. A rehearsed demo needs the same clock every time.
  • Where a model belongs: hearing the request, choosing what to draw, and checking the board from a photo. One second is fine there, outside the motion loop. SayCan, Code as Policies and Gemini Robotics-ER all split the job this way.

The benchmark to beat: the Clock Drawing Test

Doctors ask patients to draw a clock showing a set time, as a dementia screen. Two published studies gave language models the same test. Nobody has published a "draw a clock at HH:MM" benchmark, so this is the fair ground.

StudyResultFair for us?
Google, arXiv 2410.11756 (Oct 2024)12 models, WMS-IV rubric out of 15. Best 14/15. Only 2 of 12 set the hands to the right timeYes, rubric published in full
BMJ "Age against the machine" (Dec 2024)MoCA clock, "10 past 11": none of the language models fully passedYes, 3-point rubric
Davoudi et al. (2021)430 healthy adults draw it in 35.0 s on averageYes, as the human speed reference
The 10:10 problemImage models drift to the watch-advert time; one blog test found 0 of 10 fully right at 3:27Motivation only: re-run it ourselves
ClockBench (2025)Models reading clocks: best went from 13.3% to 83.3% in a yearNo: reading, not drawing

The claim, once measured: "20 random times, 20 of 20 clocks right to the minute, X seconds each. Healthy adults take 35 s; in published tests no language model passed the clinic clock."

  • Times from a seeded random generator; the same prompt for every system; current image models re-run by us on the same times.
  • Score on the MoCA and WMS-IV rubrics, graded blind by someone outside the team. Minute hand within 6°, hour hand within about 5°, measured from photos.
  • Time from request to pen-up, path planning included; 10 runs, median and range.
  • Draw 12, 3, 6, 9 first: the rubric gives 2 points for it.
  • The judge's question will be "a calculator beating a poet at sums". The answer is the thesis: for a job with a right answer, the model chooses and code draws.

The Fourier pipeline, and why it is fast

  • Pen-up travel is most of plot time. Reordering strokes "can reduce the plotting time by 30-50%" (AxiDraw docs); on one big map pen-up was about 87% of travel. One Fourier loop has no pen lifts.
  • Corners force slowdowns (Klipper caps a right angle at 5 mm/s by default). A truncated Fourier series has no corners.
  • The draw time is known before the pen moves. With the arm's speed and acceleration limits, the shortest safe time follows from the coefficients alone, so N becomes a dial between detail and seconds:

T = max( 2π·Σ|k||ck| / vmax , 2π·√( Σk²|ck| / amax ) )

Our own working from the series' derivatives. The clock face barely needs it: a circle is a one-term Fourier series, the hands are lines and the numerals come from a single-stroke font. The pipeline is for whatever the camera or microphone gives it next.

Video and voice, as data over time

The story to open with: in 1872 Lord Kelvin built a brass Fourier machine that drew the future. Ten rotating components, summed by a chain over pulleys, moved a pen that plotted tidal height against time. Machines like it predicted the tides for D-Day.

  • Voice. The phonautograph drew sound in soot in 1857. Draw a voice's waveform or pitch as one loop, then play the same path back as stereo audio, the way oscilloscope music draws pictures with sound.
  • Video. Chain a short clip's frames into one line, as the Bad Apple Fourier video did: a flip-book on the whiteboard where position along the line is time. Live: the camera sees a face and the arm draws it as one loop.
  • Data. Fit harmonics to a periodic series and draw its future, Kelvin's method. This is the forecast plotter, with a 150-year-old precedent for the HRT prize.
  • The time dial. A slider for N shows the computed draw time before the pen moves, so a judge watches detail traded for seconds.
