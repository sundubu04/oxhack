Clockwork motion planning

Only the picture comes from a model. Fixed code does the rest: it fits and orders the strokes, solves two joint angles for every point 2 mm apart, and times each move so no servo is asked for more than it can do. The servos are not what limits the speed. Drawing needs at most 83°/s from a servo rated 600°/s; the clock's 10 seconds go on the pen speed we set and on lifting the pen.

From words to servo pulses

StepWhat it does
1Picturelib/gemini.mjsGemini writes an SVG line drawing on a 100 × 100 grid. Its instructions ask for "a few continuous strokes so an arm can draw it quickly", with the least thinking the model allows. The clock and the other samples need no model; the clock is built from the time now.
2Strokessrc/svg.js, vectorize.jsSVG shapes become polylines: cubic curves at 16 points, quadratic at 12, circles and ellipses at 48. A raster picture is shrunk to 256 px, thresholded, its outlines traced, specks under 14 px dropped and the rest simplified to within 1.5 px.
3Fitpathplan.jsCentres the drawing, scales its longer side to 180 mm, flips it upright and places it at the middle of the board, 215 mm from the base.
4Orderorder()Greedy nearest neighbour: start at the stroke nearest the drawing's left corner by the base, then always go to the closest stroke end, drawing each stroke whichever way round is nearer. The clock's pen-up travel drops from 732 to 598 mm.
5Resampleresample()A waypoint every 2 mm along each stroke, so the joints never have far to go between points.
6Lead-inplan()A pen-up point 3 mm before each stroke, in the stroke's own direction, so the gears' slack is taken up before the pen lands.
7Anglesik.jsClosed-form two-link inverse kinematics, elbow up. The nib touches beside the forearm's tip, so the forearm is solved as a slightly longer link turned by a small angle. Pen-up moves aim the end that lands next. Points out of reach or past a servo's range are counted and shown.
8TimingmakeJob() in sim.jsEach move gets the longer of the pen's time and the slowest joint's time, with a 20% margin on the servo. When the tilt changes, the arm holds still until the pen has landed or lifted.
9Compensationcompensate()Each base and elbow command is pushed ahead by half the servo's dead band (1°) plus half its gear play (0 until measured), in the direction the joint is turning.
10Keyframesarm.jsAngles go out in each servo's own frame (zero offset, direction, gear ratio) as hundredths of a degree with a time stamp, into a 48-slot buffer on the board that the app never overfills.
11Arduinofirmware/clockworkMoves the servos between keyframes on its own 20 ms servo frame, so the laptop's timing never shows in the line.

None of the 432 moves was slowed by the servos. Lifting the pen is only a 4.8° turn of the tilt servo (from −57.3° to −52.4°), about 8 ms at full speed; the other 50 ms of each wait is a fixed settle in the schedule.

How the servos' speed is respected

Every move between two waypoints gets whichever time is longer:

move time = max(distance ÷ pen speed, 1.2 × biggest joint turn ÷ servo speed) tilt wait = tilt turn ÷ servo speed + 50 ms, with the arm still

So a servo is never asked to turn faster than 83% of the speed we tell the app it has. The pen speed is 150 mm/s drawing and 300 mm/s pen up; the servo speed is the SG92R datasheet's 0.1 s per 60° at 4.8 V, which is 600°/s.

The joints have little to do because the arm is long: at 215 mm out, one degree of base turn moves the pen almost 4 mm, so 150 mm/s at the pen is a slow turn for the servo. The other half of the work is done by the Arduino, which moves each servo smoothly between keyframes 50 times a second instead of jumping from point to point.

If the servos are slower than the datasheet

Likely, with a 180 mm link and a marker on the horn. The slowdown itself costs little. What breaks the line is planning for the wrong speed.

Down to 200°/s the only cost is slower pen lifts. Below 150°/s the drawing itself slows, and if the app still thinks the servo does 600°/s the pen lands before the arm arrives. Planned for the real speed, every row stays within 0.1 mm in the sim.

  • Measure it on the arm. With the links, the pen and the real 5 V supply on, send J commands 60° apart from the Serial Monitor and film the horn in slow motion. Frames from start to stop ÷ the frame rate = seconds per 60°.
  • Enter it in Arm setup as Servo speed. The plan re-times itself; only the moves that need it slow down.

Keeping the line on the plan

A hobby servo stops half its dead band short of where it is told, and gear play leaves the link behind the motor. The sim models both; compensation and the lead-in take them out.

The lead-in costs 0.13 s on the clock. The model's dead band is exact and the same every time; a real servo's is not, so expect a clear gain on the board, not 0.1 mm.
