# Pitch craft

What great startup decks and demos do, kept from the research behind darling-agentic's drl-pitch-deck (compiled 22 Oct 2026 from web sources; every claim carries a source number, listed in the last section). Written for investor decks; at a hackathon, the judges are the investors and the demo is the traction. "(secondary)" means a third-party summary that could not be checked against the primary text.

## (a) Rules for a deck builder

1. **Say what you do in the first sentence and on slide 1, in words a stranger can repeat.** PG: "Say what you're doing as soon as possible, preferably in the first sentence" [5]. Seibel: "Start with the name of your company and what it does. There's no need to set up the problem" and run the "email test" (two sentences to a smart friend, who must explain it back) [3]. Sequoia: "define your company in a single declarative sentence" [9]. Ralston: "We deliver groceries to customers in their homes" beats jargon [2]. DocSend 2022: the purpose slide is now used as a quick gatekeeper and was the third most-viewed section [11]. Papermark 2024-25: page 1 gets about 23 s versus about 15 s for each later page (data internally inconsistent) [17]. Seibel's YC-interview warning: "a minute into the interview, we don't know what they do" (secondary) [3b].

2. **One idea per slide; the headline is a full-sentence claim, the body is the evidence.** Hale: "A simple slide, therefore, expresses one idea"; state the conclusion explicitly and caption graphs so nobody has to study them [1]. Hoffman: titles should carry the thesis [19]. Jarvis faults Airbnb, Front and Alan for label headers ("Market Validation", "Problem") instead of headlines that make the point [33a, 36c, 40]. Lab evidence: assertion-evidence slides (sentence headline plus visual evidence) beat topic-title-plus-bullets on comprehension and one-week recall (Garner and Alley 2013, p < .01; small engineering-student samples) [26].

3. **Build two decks: a presented deck with almost no words and a sent deck that reads on its own.** Presented: PG "under 20 words if you can" [5]; Ralston: more than about seven words is probably too many [2]; Kawasaki: no font under 30 pt [27]. Sent: DocSend's dataset averages about 20 pages and about 50 words per slide [14]. Investors spend under 3 minutes on a sent deck [14]; seed viewing time fell below 2 minutes in 2023 [16]; they abandon failing decks after 2 min 13 s (2022) [11].

4. **Keep it short and skip the contents page.** Successful pre-seed decks averaged 16 slides versus 19 for unsuccessful ones (DocSend 2023, via secondary) [15]. 500 Global: "A well-crafted pitch deck doesn't need more than 10 slides" [18]. Hale: pick the 5 to 7 ideas that matter [1]. Successful decks in DocSend's data had no table of contents [14]. YC Demo Day is now one slide and one minute [7, 8].

5. **Open with the investment thesis and close on it.** Hoffman: state the 3 to 8 beliefs an investor must hold, support them, end on a thesis slide, not "Appendix" or "Thanks" [19]. Ralston: build the talk on 3 or 4 "vertebrae" and list them again at the end; "Insist that your audience remember you" [2]. Jarvis on Front's closing "thanks" slide: replace with a summary or thesis [36c].

6. **Show the product early, before the long problem section.** Successful 2023 pre-seed decks put product and business model near the start, before problem and solution; unsuccessful ones put product mid-deck and often led with team (DocSend 2023 playbook, search excerpt; page blocked) [16]. DocSend 2015: the winning order was Sequoia's with product and team moved earlier [10]. 500 Global: introduce the product on slide 3 "or you risk losing your audience's attention" [18]. Coinbase put live product screens on its title slide [39a].

7. **Steer into the biggest objection early, and name your risks yourself.** Hoffman: "Steer into your investors' objections" in the first slides; "Explicitly identify the risks that could thwart your success and how you will mitigate them" [19].

8. **Traction: one metric, one chart, current value plus growth rate, with the takeaway in the caption.** 500 Global: "Typically, you would only show one metric of growth and put it in a chart", with MoM growth [18]. Ralston: avoid cumulative graphs (they always go up and invite suspicion) and complex graphs [2]. Seibel at pre-seed wants the ratio of progress to time spent, and values product and customers above fundraising or BD deals [3]. Hustle Fund: "anything is better than 0" [25]. Never inflate: Polovets cites "$1 trillion" markets and annualising one week's revenue as instant credibility killers [29]; Ralston calls exaggeration "fatal" [2].

9. **Give a unique, durable insight, not just a pain point.** Hudson (Precursor): "You need to have a unique and durable insight that will still be true in 12 to 18 months"; at pre-seed, centre the story on what you know from experience [24]. Seibel calls the insight slide the most likely source of an investor's "aha" (Gmail: the inbox is a personal database, so never delete) [3].

10. **Make "why now" an explicit slide, framed as a shift in the world.** Sequoia lists "Why now?" as its own section [9]. DocSend: VCs spent 65% more time on "Why now?" in 2023 and successful decks foregrounded it [12]; DocSend says it matters at pre-seed more than at Series A [14]. Raskin's Zuora analysis: open with a big, relevant change in the world (the "subscription economy"), then position the problem as an obstacle to thriving in the new world [46b]. Vertical-AI tailwinds investors already believe: Bessemer (vertical AI can reach legal, healthcare, finance that legacy SaaS could not) [44a]; YC Lightcone "Vertical AI agents could be 10x bigger than SaaS" (Nov 2024, secondary) [45].

11. **Size the market bottom-up, show the arithmetic, keep it to one slide.** Seibel prefers bottom-up because top-down fails to narrow the customer base [3]. Ralston: "We can sell for $X, and we have Y potential customers"; avoid "1% of a huge market" [2]. 500 Global accepts either but wants a path to more than $1B [18]. Pre-seed market-size slides got 19% less attention in 2024 [13], and PG says Demo Day pitches need not give market size at all [5]. If you are creating a market, argue expansion like Gurley on Uber ("it's not about the market that exists, it's about the market we're creating") but still show the maths [21]. Cautionary tale: Airbnb reportedly changed its market slide from millions to billions on Sam Altman's advice (secondary) and assumed an unjustified 15% share [33c, 33e].

12. **Competition: name real alternatives, including the status quo, and say in one line what each lacks.** PG: "list the top 3 and explain in one sentence each what they lack that you have" [5]. Never claim no competition: Hoffman [19], 500 Global ("saying you're the 'first' ... often shows a lack of knowledge") [18], DocSend (a missing competition slide reads as "a not-so-subtle indicator") [11]. Successful 2023 seed decks got 88% more time on competition [12]. A 2x2 is fine only if the axes are the buyer's criteria and you can defend them; Hunter Walk has never seen a startup outside the top right and asks what competitors would say about the axes: "Otherwise you're better off leaving it out" [20].

13. **Pre-empt "isn't this a feature of X?" with the incumbent's incentives, not bravado.** Reasons incumbents do not build it: market too small for them, poor organisational fit, and counter-positioning ("actively cannibalizes core business") [22]. Give the specific gap (workflow, segment, integration) and what you do if they enter [22]. For services-shaped AI, investors also ask Andrusko's questions: "Show me an opinionated platform boundary", engineer-months from contract to production, year-3 margins, what breaks at 50 customers, how you decide not to customise [41c].

14. **One business model, stated simply.** Seibel: commit to one simple model; his Justin.tv mistake was offering many [3]. Hoffman: several revenue streams can signal lack of focus [19]. PG: "any business model you have at this point is probably wrong anyway", so do not dwell [5].

15. **Team slide: why this team for this problem, and how the founders fit together.** Seibel's list: number of founders, technical co-founder, how long they have known each other, full-time, roughly equal equity, relevant expertise for complex fields [3]. Founder/market fit: founders with deep understanding of the market who "personify their product" (Dixon 2011, coining credited to David Lee) [23]. Hudson: without traction, investors weigh how well the founder knows the industry [24]. 500 Global: "One or two brief callouts per key team member" [18]. Pre-seed team slides got 30% more time in 2024, seed 40% [13]. Skip degrees and adjectives: Jarvis on Airbnb (degrees matter little) [33a]; PG: "Never say 'we're passionate'" [5]; Jarvis on Front ("Mix of passion and experience" is meaningless) [36c]. YC's founder video exists to show founder dynamics, all founders on camera [6].

16. **Customer proof: named logos and usage numbers beat testimonial walls.** 500 Global: B2B companies can add client logos to traction [18]. Ralston: testimonial-style slides have little persuasive value; read any quote aloud [2]. Hoffman: internal data beats third-party anecdotes [19]. Legora's announcements pair elite named firms with counts (250 firms in 20 markets, then 400+ in 40+) [34a, 34b]. Jarvis on Front: quotes are "easy to obtain", seat counts per client are better [36c].

17. **Make the ask exact and tie it to a milestone.** Seibel: "There's no need to dance around the ask" [3]. Airbnb: $500K for 12 months and 80,000 transactions [33d]. Hoffman: always plan for the next round; underpromise and overdeliver [19].

18. **End with a vision you can say in one breath: wedge, then expansion.** Sequoia: what you will have built in five years [9]. Sequoia's Harvey announcement: lawyers, then professional services, then all knowledge work ("AI super app", secondary) [35b]. Front's Series C "very ambitious long-term vision" was slide 23 of a metrics-heavy deck [36b].

19. **Design for the back of the room: big type, high contrast, key text at the top, nothing decorative.** Hale: avoid excess text, caveats, heavy branding, uncaptioned photos, animations, transitions, memes; apply the stranger test [1]. Ralston: large fonts, high contrast, important text near the top [2]. Polovets: typos are "a yellow flag" [29]. Jarvis: remove interstitial slides, dates and emojis [40].

20. **The deck's job is to win the next meeting, not the cheque.** Ralston: "intrigue your listeners enough that they will want to meet you" [2]. Hustle Fund: "Pitch decks will never convince someone to invest" (DeMars) [25]. Hale: "Investors invest in teams not slides" [1]. Airbnb's deck drew five rejections and two non-replies in June 2008 [33f].

21. **Pitch by analogy, never reason by it.** Hoffman: "It's better to have no analogy than a bad one" [19]. Coinbase's "iTunes for bitcoin" framing is credited (by a commentator) with landing the category [39a]; Airbnb's tagline "Book rooms with locals, rather than hotels" does the same job [33d].

22. **Candour is a credibility asset.** PG: "You can afford to be candid about what you haven't figured out yet" [5]. Jarvis praises Front's cohort slide for admitting a failed Stripe integration and a customer shutdown [36c]. Uber's 2008 deck gave worst, realistic and best cases [38a].

23. **One presenter, rehearsed to memory.** Ralston: one presenter chosen for clarity, not title [2]. PG: one or at most two speakers; "Err on the side of speaking slowly" [5]. Polovets: "for a 120-second talk, just memorize it" [29].

24. **Have every number ready, and an appendix for objections.** Collin: "make sure you have all the metrics investors might ask for so you can quickly and accurately respond" [30]; metrics without a narrative arc are a common failure [30]. Hoffman: prepare appendix slides to answer likely objections [19].

## (d) Demo guidance

**When.**
- In a partner meeting: demo straight after the one-liner. PG: "A demo explains what you've made more effectively than any verbal description" [5]; Weebly did it in the first five minutes [30]; Intercom saved its product reveal for the demo [37b]. Some investors try the product before meeting (Lemkin, secondary), so send a working sandbox link ahead [47].
- On a big stage or at Demo Day: no live demo. Ralston: live demos "seldom work" [2]; PG marks his own "get rapidly to demo" advice obsolete for Demo Day [5]; Polovets: "A demo is nice if it works, but embarrassing ... if it doesn't"; use teaser images and offer a 1:1 demo afterwards [29].
- Applications: YC wants the demo in its own field, never in the founder video [6].

**Format.**
| Format | Use when | Risk | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| Live | Partner meeting, product reliable, the live moment is the point | Failure in the room; have a backup machine and connection | [28] |
| Recorded clip | Covering ground fast, product still buggy, or showing a real customer interaction | Feels canned; edit out login/onboarding | [28, 29] |
| Canned-but-live (seeded data, local build) | Stage or flaky network | Can look staged; worth it to make the point | [28, 29] |
| Stills in the deck | Sent deck, Demo Day slide | Screenshots are usually illegible: enlarge one region and caption it | [1] |

DocSend advises spreading product across 3 to 5 slides with wireframes or screenshots in pre-seed decks (search excerpt) [16b].

**Structure (Rob Go, NextView, Jan 2026) [28].**
1. Start with who the user is, then what the investor is looking at.
2. Aim for "1 or 2 exclamation marks"; if the investor will not be impressed, do not demo.
3. Skip sign-up and generic onboarding; keep tabs open to jump ahead.
4. Make the edge visible: show canned examples or the reasoning trail behind an output; "it's more important to hammer home your point."
5. Do not show something unpolished with an apology; demo only if the function is striking even when rough.

**The first 30 seconds.** No single source states an "aha in 30 seconds" rule; it is a synthesis. YC compresses Demo Day to one minute [7, 8]; Seibel's interview warning is that a minute in, partners often still do not know what the company does (secondary) [3b]; page 1 of a sent deck gets the most attention [17]. So the opening of the demo should be the transformation itself (before, ask, after), not set-up.

**Proof it works.** Harvey's first "demo" was a ratio judged by outsiders (86 of 100 answers client-ready) [35a]. Legora leads with usage counts and named firms [34a, 34b]. Bessemer: show dollars or hours saved [44b].

**Strong demo moments to borrow from.** Weebly rebuilding the investor's own site [30]; Dropbox's audience-tailored screencast [32]; Figma's prototype carrying a raise without metrics [31a]; Coinbase putting live product screens on slide 1 [39a]; Airbnb's dated real screenshots [33d].
